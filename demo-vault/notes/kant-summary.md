# Kant: a 5-minute summary

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) is one of the central figures of modern philosophy. His ethics is grounded in *reason* rather than consequences or feelings.

## The categorical imperative

The core idea. An action is morally right only if you could will the rule behind it to be a universal law that everyone follows. Lying to get out of trouble fails the test: if everyone lied when convenient, promises and language itself would lose meaning, and the very act of "lying to get out of trouble" would be impossible.

He gives a second, equivalent formulation: *act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in another, always as an end and never merely as a means*. People are not tools to be used for some other goal — their rational agency demands respect.

## Duty vs. inclination

Kant separates moral worth from outcome and from emotion. Helping a friend because you genuinely like them is fine, but it isn't moral in the deep sense — you'd help them anyway. Helping a stranger you have no warm feelings toward, *because the moral law demands it*, is the paradigm case of moral action. The will doing its duty out of respect for the law is what counts.

## Why this matters

Kantian ethics is the main rival to utilitarianism in modern moral philosophy. Where utilitarians ask "what produces the most good?", Kantians ask "what could be willed as a universal rule?". The disagreement shows up in cases like lying to a murderer at the door (Kant says no, classical utilitarians say yes) or harvesting one person's organs to save five (Kant says no, naive utilitarians struggle to say no).

## Critiques worth knowing

- **Rigidity**: the universalizability test produces counterintuitive answers in edge cases (the murderer-at-the-door case is the famous one).
- **Empty formalism**: critics like Hegel argued the categorical imperative is too abstract to actually decide most real cases.
- **Cultural blind spots**: Kant's own writing on race and gender is genuinely awful and has prompted sustained debate about whether the framework can be cleanly separated from its author.
