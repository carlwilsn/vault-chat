# Utilitarianism: a 5-minute summary

Utilitarianism judges actions by their consequences — specifically, by how much overall happiness or well-being they produce. Bentham (1748–1832) and Mill (1806–1873) are the founding figures.

## The principle

*The right action is the one that produces the greatest good for the greatest number.* Pleasure is the only intrinsic good; pain is the only intrinsic bad. Everything else — honesty, freedom, justice — matters only insofar as it tends to produce more pleasure or less pain in the long run.

## Bentham vs. Mill

Bentham was a quantitative hedonist: pleasure is pleasure, whether it's poetry or pushpin (a child's game). Add up the units, pick the action with the biggest sum.

Mill pushed back. He distinguished *higher pleasures* (intellectual, aesthetic) from *lower pleasures* (bodily). His test: anyone who has experienced both will prefer the higher. *"Better to be a human dissatisfied than a pig satisfied."* The qualitative move tries to rescue utilitarianism from the charge that it's a philosophy fit for swine.

## Act vs. rule

- **Act utilitarianism**: each individual action is judged by its consequences.
- **Rule utilitarianism**: we follow rules whose general adoption maximizes utility, even if a particular act under the rule has worse consequences than the alternative.

Rule utilitarianism handles the standard counterexamples (framing an innocent man to prevent a riot, harvesting organs from one to save five) more comfortably — the rules *"don't frame innocents"* and *"don't murder patients"* have better long-run consequences than their alternatives.

## Standard objections

- **Demandingness**: if I should always maximize total utility, I'm obligated to give nearly everything to people poorer than me. Most people find this conclusion unbearable, even if they can't say what's wrong with the argument.
- **Rights and justice**: a strict consequentialist seems forced to permit great harms to a few if they produce slightly greater goods for many.
- **Measurement**: how do you compare your headache to my joy, or one person's pleasure to another's pain? Utilitarianism needs interpersonal comparisons of utility, and there's no obvious way to ground them.

## The contemporary descendants

Effective altruism (Singer, MacAskill, the GiveWell crowd) is the most visible modern application. The arguments about AI safety, factory farming, and global health charity prioritization mostly run on utilitarian rails, even when the speakers wouldn't call themselves capital-U Utilitarians.
